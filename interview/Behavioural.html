Ambiguity and Career Progression

A junior-level developer is expected to do a task, while a mid-level engineer operates more at a feature level with sub-tasks and is expected to break these sub-tasks down. This is the first glimpse into ambiguity. A junior just does tasks, but a mid-level engineer is expected to break down the feature into sub-tasks. Now at a more senior level, we may be operating on a project that might span multiple months and involve other people with other lists of sub-tasks that might be delegated to other mid-level engineers. The ability to organize those sub-tasks and coach those mid-level engineers is what we're looking for in a senior engineer. The level of ambiguity now has increased. Ambiguity increases with the level of compensation. So a CEO of a big organization has a very ambiguous job responsibility. We have tasks, features, projects, and goals. The staff-level person or manager who has team responsibility is thinking about the goals. What should be the goal for the period of time we're operating on? And how do we get to those goals broken down into projects and delegate those to engineers? So if I have to answer a question of this type at any level, and I pick a task or a feature to answer these types of questions, I need to explain how I handled that ambiguity. Don't just talk about the good part of the task or the most interesting part, such as the technology in use, but how did you break that down? Did you talk to anybody else? Did you make a list? Did you use a task system available to you? These are the kind of repeatable answers a great hiring manager is looking for. So I never will build again a system I have built in the past. I need to state the actions I took when I undertook those projects in the past so I can take a similar approach in the future, so I can do it on the new team.

Starting with Ambiguity

Sometimes, a behavioral question might feel ambiguous, or you might not have a perfect, direct example. Don't panic! The key is to take control of the situation and clarify. A simple explanation is to reframe the question. You can start by asking a clarifying question to narrow down the scope or to ensure you understand what the interviewer is looking for. This shows you're a thoughtful problem-solver, not just someone who recites memorized answers.

Sample Question & Answer: Handling Ambiguity

Question: How do you handle ambiguity?

S - Situation: "In a previous role as a project coordinator, I was assigned a new project to improve our internal document management system. The initial brief was very high-level and lacked specific details on scope, budget, and timeline."

T - Task: "My task was to take this ambiguous brief and turn it into a clear, actionable project plan that the team could execute."

A - Action: "I started by organizing meetings with key stakeholders from different departments to gather their requirements and understand their pain points. I created a document with a list of clarifying questions and sent it to the stakeholders beforehand. Based on their feedback, I then drafted a preliminary project scope and a list of key deliverables. I scheduled a follow-up meeting to present this draft and get final sign-off before proceeding with a detailed plan."

R - Result: "My proactive approach transformed the ambiguous request into a well-defined project. The clear plan led to a 15% reduction in project delays and a 20% increase in stakeholder satisfaction because their needs were clearly understood from the start."

More Sample Questions & Answers

Question: Tell me about a time you had to deal with a difficult team member.

S - Situation: "In a group project, one of my team members was consistently missing deadlines, which impacted the rest of the team's ability to complete their tasks on time."

T - Task: "My task was to address the issue and find a way to get the project back on track without causing conflict within the team."

A - Action: "Instead of immediately bringing the issue to the whole team or a manager, I scheduled a one-on-one conversation with the team member. I approached it from a place of support, asking if they were okay and if there was anything I could do to help. I learned they were struggling with a heavy workload from another project. Together, we created a new, more manageable timeline for their tasks and I offered to take on a small portion of their work to ease the burden."

R - Result: "This open and honest conversation led to an immediate improvement. The team member was able to meet the new deadlines, and we completed the project on time and with a high degree of quality. It also strengthened our team's communication and trust."

Sample Question & Answer: Conflict Resolution

Question: Tell me about a time you solved a conflict.

S - Situation: "On a recent project, a disagreement arose between two team members regarding the technical approach for a key feature. One person favored an established, reliable method, while the other wanted to try a newer, more efficient technology. This led to a stalemate, and the project was stalled."

T - Task: "My task was to mediate the disagreement, find a resolution, and get the team back on track with the project timeline."

A - Action: "I first met with each team member individually to understand their perspective fully, listening to their reasoning without taking sides. I then organized a group meeting and acted as a neutral facilitator, encouraging them to present their arguments in a structured way. Instead of choosing one over the other, I proposed a compromise: we would use the reliable method for the initial phase of the project to meet the deadline, but we would also dedicate a small portion of time for a proof-of-concept using the newer technology. This way, we could validate the new approach for future projects."

R - Result: "This approach successfully resolved the conflict. Both team members felt heard and valued, and the project was able to move forward. The proof-of-concept later proved that the new technology was viable, and we successfully adopted it for the next project, leading to a 10% increase in development speed. The team learned the importance of communicating openly and finding creative compromises."

Practice with More Examples

  • Tell me about a time you had to make a decision without a lot of information.
  • Describe a project you worked on where you faced a significant challenge. How did you handle it?
  • Give me an example of when you had to work with a team member you didn't get along with.
  • Tell me about a time you had to make a decision without a lot of information.
