In real-life payment reconciliation systems, editing directly in the reconciliation dashboard is generally very limited or avoided because:

Data integrity and audit trails are critical — changing transaction records manually can cause serious accounting issues.

Source of truth usually resides in backend systems or databases controlled by payment processors, banks, or ERP software.

Corrections typically happen via separate workflows, like issuing refunds, chargebacks, or adjusting accounting entries — not by editing raw transaction records in the dashboard itself.

Users mostly review, investigate mismatches, and trigger downstream processes (e.g., alerts, reports, or tickets) rather than edit data directly.

So, what happens in real systems?

The reconciliation dashboard is mainly read-only and used for monitoring and investigation.

When a mismatch is found, users initiate a process outside the dashboard to correct or resolve issues.

Some advanced systems allow annotations, comments, or status updates to track investigations, but not direct edits to financial data.

Final data updates happen in the authoritative backend systems, with strict controls and audit logging.
